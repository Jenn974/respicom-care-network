5 Signs Your Elderly Parent May Need In-Home Care

Understanding changes in everyday life and exploring suitable support options.

Home Care and Family Support

Educational article · Respicom Care Network

It can be difficult for families to know when an older parent or loved one may need additional help at home. Some changes happen gradually, so they may be easy to overlook. Paying attention, listening respectfully and discussing concerns with the person can help families decide whether more support is needed.

Here are five signs that may be worth discussing with your loved one and, when appropriate, a qualified healthcare professional.

1. Difficulty with Everyday Activities

Your loved one may begin struggling with activities they previously managed independently, such as preparing meals, getting dressed, bathing, shopping or keeping up with household tasks.

Ask what has become difficult and what kind of assistance the person would find helpful. A healthcare professional can help assess changes in daily functioning and possible support needs.

2. Changes in Personal Care or Household Conditions

You might notice that personal hygiene routines have changed, laundry is piling up, food is going unused or the home has become harder to maintain.

These changes can have many causes, including illness, mobility difficulties, fatigue or lack of practical support. Approach the conversation with respect and avoid making assumptions about the cause.

3. Mobility Difficulties or Safety Concerns

Difficulty walking, getting up from a chair, using stairs or moving around the home may make everyday activities more challenging. Falls or repeated near-falls should be taken seriously.

Consider whether the home environment needs assessment for safety and whether a qualified healthcare professional should review changes in mobility. Sudden weakness, sudden confusion or other severe, unexpected symptoms require urgent medical assessment.

4. Changes in Memory or Daily Routines

You may notice missed appointments, forgotten tasks, confusion about familiar routines or difficulty managing usual responsibilities.

Memory changes can have different causes and do not automatically mean that someone has dementia. Encourage a medical assessment when changes are new, persistent or affecting daily life. Sudden confusion requires urgent medical attention.

5. Social Withdrawal or Difficulty Managing Alone

An older person may stop participating in activities they previously enjoyed, have difficulty attending appointments or seem to be finding daily life increasingly challenging.

Ask how they are feeling and whether practical help, transport, companionship or a review of their care arrangements might be useful. Respect their preferences and involve them in decisions about their support wherever possible.

What should families do next?

Start with a calm, respectful conversation. Listen to your loved one's wishes, write down the practical difficulties you have observed and seek professional advice when appropriate. Explore care arrangements based on the person's needs, preferences, safety and available resources.
